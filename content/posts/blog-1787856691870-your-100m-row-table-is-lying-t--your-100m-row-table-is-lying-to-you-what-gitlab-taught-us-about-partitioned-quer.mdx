You have a PostgreSQL table with 100 million rows, partitioned by date. You add an index. And still, that date-range query crawls. Sound familiar? You are not alone — GitLab hit this exact wall when its database ballooned past 5 TB and a single global index turned full-text search into an 8-second nightmare [1]. Here is the uncomfortable truth: the answer is never just "add an index." Let's walk the path from diagnostic to fix, using GitLab's own battlefield as our map.

## Hook — The 8-Second Search That Should Take Milliseconds

Picture this: a developer types a plain word — 'foobar' — into your issue tracker's search bar, and waits. And waits. Two point eight seconds later, the results shuffle onto the screen. For a group-scoped search, that wait stretches to a full eight seconds. That is not a lag spike; that is the sound of a database quietly suffocating. This was the reality at GitLab.com, where the issues table alone held 23 million rows and the team was paying the price for a single global 19 GB GIN trigram index sitting on a massively spread-out heap [1]. The stakes are simple: every extra hundred milliseconds is a developer yanked out of flow, and at GitLab's scale, those milliseconds add up to real human cost.

## Problem — Partitioned, Indexed, and Still Painfully Slow

Here is the classic trap. The table is partitioned by date. The query filters on a date range. The obvious fix should work: create an index, let the planner prune the partitions, and reap the speedup. Yet the query stays slow, and the team stares at the EXPLAIN output wondering what went wrong. The truth is that partitioning alone rarely fixes slow queries, and an index only helps if the planner actually uses it. Many developers assume the partition key is doing all the heavy lifting, but the geometry of your data — how rows are physically laid out, whether the planner can prune early, and whether the query needs expensive sorts — matters far more than the presence of an index. The problem is a combination: pruning that only partially happens, indexes that the planner ignores in favor of sequential scans, and sort operations that eat your memory budget whole.

## Real-World Case — GitLab

This is where the theory gets real. GitLab's PostgreSQL database grew past 5 TB, and individual tables soared beyond 100 GB [1]. The full-text search over the issues table — built on a single global 19 GB GIN trigram index — ground to a halt. A group-scoped issue search took eight seconds, and a simple title/description lookup took nearly three. The root cause was a perfect storm: one monolithic index, physical data sprawled across a huge heap, and no way for the planner to take advantage of the partitioned layout. The fix was to move from one global index to per-partition trigram indexes. The results were dramatic: group issue search dropped from about 2 seconds to about 0.2 seconds on a warm cache — an 8-10x speedup — and each per-partition index became 10-100x smaller than the old global index [1]. The trade-off? Query planning time rose from 10ms to 40ms due to the extra partition metadata. Nobody mentions the 'but' when they quote the speedup — but that planning cost is exactly the kind of nuance that separates a good engineer from a great one.

## Deep Dive — Partition Pruning, Index Utilization, and the Sort That Eats You

So what should you actually look at in that EXPLAIN plan? Start with partition pruning. You want to confirm that the planner is only touching the partitions that actually contain your date range — not scanning all of them out of caution. If pruning is missing or partial, no index will save you. Next, examine index utilization: is the planner doing an index scan, or falling back to a sequential scan because the query is too unselective, or because the statistics are stale? Then hunt for the expensive operations — sorts and hash aggregates that balloon your memory and row estimates. This leads to the deeper insight: the fix often is not one optimization but a composition of them. That is why a composite index on (date, filtered_columns) is such a common win — it lets the planner satisfy both the partition key and the WHERE clause in a single pass. And when physical layout is still poorly ordered, CLUSTER can reorder the heap so that the index and the data agree on where rows live, cutting random I/O dramatically.

## Workflow — The Diagnostic Checklist from Pain to Speedup

Building on all that, here is the repeatable workflow that emerges from GitLab's experience. The diagram below maps the journey. First, run an EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) on the real query and confirm whether partition pruning is firing. Second, check whether the planner is using an index or a sequential scan — if it is scanning, ask why. Third, identify the expensive sorts and aggregates at the top of the plan. Fourth, design a composite index that covers the WHERE clause and matches your pruning key. Fifth, evaluate clustering when data access patterns demand it. Finally, measure before and after — because the trade-offs, like GitLab's uptick in planning time, only reveal themselves when you benchmark the whole system. This workflow is the difference between guessing and diagnosing.

## Lessons Learned — What to Do Differently Tomorrow

If this feels overwhelming, you are not alone. Every one of these lessons is hard-won, and they all distill into a handful of principles. Partitioning is a tool, not a silver bullet — it only pays off when the planner actually prunes. An index is only as good as the plan that uses it, so verify with EXPLAIN instead of assuming. The cheapest optimization in the world is knowing whether you are sorting, scanning, or pruning before you write any migration. And most importantly, measure the whole system, not just the happy path — because, as GitLab discovered, a 10ms planner hiccup can be the hidden tax on a 10x speedup. Tomorrow, before touching a single column, run EXPLAIN on the slow query and look for those three red flags: no pruning, no index, and an expensive sort.

## Conclusion

So what should you do differently tomorrow? Stop trusting indexes and partitioning on faith — trust the EXPLAIN plan. On your next slow query, run EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS), confirm pruning fires, confirm the index is used, and hunt the sort that eats your memory. Build composite indexes that match your partition key, reach for CLUSTER when the heap fights you, and always measure the whole system before and after. The one insight to share with your team: a 10x speedup almost always ships with a hidden tax somewhere — GitLab's was 30ms of planning time. Find yours before it finds you.
